A corrugator engineer values International Paper

I've stood next to the machine that makes this company's product for eleven years. I'd never valued the company. the story first

Damodaran's rule is that every valuation is a story with numbers attached, and the story comes first, because the numbers are only as good as the story they're consistent with. Here's the story as I see it from the plant, before I open a spreadsheet.

the bull case: capacity out, price in
  • IP closed Savannah (1.0 million tons a year) and Riceboro (430k) in September 2025, after Red River and Orange. The industry took out 3.9 million tons in 2025 — nearly 10% of North American containerboard capacity.
  • Three price increases in 2026. The index has recognized about $170 a ton on linerboard. The CFO's own sensitivity: every $10 a ton is $90M of EBITDA a year.
  • 80/20 found ~$510M of run-rate cost in 2025 and expects the same in 2026. Thirty-one EMEA closures, 2,800 positions.
  • They bought the NORPAC mill in Washington for $360M because, in the CEO's words, they were "significantly short paper on the West Coast and shipping it there uneconomically." Anyone who's watched a truck of linerboard cross three states knows what that costs.
the bear case: expensive assets, soft demand
  • Demand is flat. The trade forecast for 2026 containerboard went from +2% to −2% after February. The bank analysts are "mostly worried about demand."
  • EMEA (the DS Smith business) lost $80M in Q2 because paper prices rose faster than box prices. That lag is real; I've lived it from the other side.
  • The 10% capacity cut was cost economics — low performers out — not a demand squeeze. Those mills aren't coming back, which supports price, but the tightness is thinner than the headline.
  • Paper is capital-hungry. D&A is 8% of sales; capex is running $2.1B a year. The dividend costs $980M and 2025 free cash flow was negative. The 5.8% yield is the market telling you it doesn't believe the dividend is safe.

And one thing that isn't in either column because it happened this week: IP announced in January it would spin off EMEA, and on October 15 it said it had unsolicited bids and was running a sale process in parallel. The DS Smith capital markets day is November 3. So the question "what is the sum of the parts worth" is no longer academic.

narrative to numbers

A DCF is a machine that turns operating assumptions into a price. The machine is standard; the assumptions are everything. So here is 2027 built as a bridge from 2026 guidance, with each piece tied to the story, and with my own judgments marked as judgments.

The 2027 bridge for the North American business, $ millions. Grey is company guidance; orange are my judgments (the model's yellow cells); green is what falls out — and the deliberate step down to mid-cycle.

The judgments, stated plainly so you can disagree with them:

  • Half the price sticks. Of the $170 a ton the index has recognized, I assume 50% holds at the box level through 2027. Index-linked contracts lag by months; the third round hasn't reached the open market yet; a soft year gives some of it back. Half, net of the $175M already in 2026 guidance, is about $590M of 2027 EBITDA.
  • Volume up 1%. The market is flat-to-down; IP says it's outperforming by three or four points. I split the difference and assume a 30% contribution margin on the increment, which is what a box plant running through existing mills earns on the next ton.
  • $300M of cost-out against $250M of inflation. OCC is back to $80–100 a ton from the 2025 lows, freight is up, and 80/20 keeps finding money. Net of each other, it's roughly a wash — which is what a transformation looks like from the P&L two years in.
  • Then it comes back down. This is the Damodaran part people skip. 2027 is a price-increase year, and a price-increase year is a peak. A cyclical gets valued at mid-cycle, not at the peak. So 2028 onward I use a mid-cycle NA margin of 17% — between Smurfit Westrock's 15% and Packaging Corp's 21% — and the model deliberately shows EBITDA stepping down from 2027 to 2028. If that looks wrong to you, that's the assumption to argue with.

That's the thing the model made me see. Back out what the price implies and the stock trades at 7.7× 2026 guidance and about 6× the 2027 bridge. The market isn't disagreeing with the arithmetic; it's disagreeing with the story. It assumes 80/20 doesn't earn IP a multiple above SW's, that most of the $170 doesn't stick, and that the dividend gets cut. Those are three versions of one belief: that the cycle turns down before the transformation shows up.

the bet, priced

A point estimate of $40 is not a bet; it's a sentence. A bet needs a distribution. So I ran the same chain twenty thousand times with the decisive assumptions drawn from ranges instead of set to values — how much price sticks, the mid-cycle margin, the exit multiple, the discount rate, volume, capex intensity — plus a 15% noise term on the terminal value for the possibility that the model's structure is simply wrong.

The first version said 90% of outcomes were above $32. That was a lie, and a common one: I'd drawn the assumptions independently, and in a downturn they move together — price doesn't stick, demand falls, margins compress and the multiple shrinks all at once. So the second version draws the cycle first, with a 30% chance of a down-cycle over the horizon, and draws everything else conditional on it.

Then the question that matters: what does the probability of a down-cycle have to be for the bet to be fair?

The whole bet is one number. P(value > $32) as the probability of a containerboard down-cycle over the next three years goes from 10% to 70%.
At $32 the market is pricing a coin flip on a down-cycle. If you think it's 30%, the shares are worth about $39 and you have an edge. If you think it's 50%, you're being paid nothing.

Everything else — the DCF, the comps, the sum of the parts — collapses into that one judgment. Which is the useful thing about doing this properly: it tells you what you're actually betting on, and it isn't the dividend or the spin-off or the price increase. It's the cycle. And the cycle is the thing you can watch: box shipments, the index in October and November, OCC, and what the DS Smith buyers are willing to pay on November 3.

On sizing: Kelly on this distribution says a half-Kelly position of about 40% of a portfolio, which is absurd, and the absurdity is the lesson. Kelly assumes you know the distribution. The distribution is the thing you're least sure of — I assigned that 30% myself. Treat Kelly as an upper bound, then divide by the number of things you could be wrong about. For one stock in one cyclical industry, that's a small position or none.

Here's where I've landed, as a person and not as advice. The honest version of this stock is a bet on the containerboard cycle, with a 5.8% yield that may or may not survive, a separation that may or may not unlock value, and a management that has been doing the hard, expensive, unpopular work of closing the mills everyone knew should close. my bet, as instruments

The model values the business; a position pays on the price, and the two can disagree for a long time. So this adds one more assumption — over about a year the price closes 60% of the gap toward the simulated value, with 12% of market noise on top — and maps that onto the four ways I could actually express the view, per 100 shares. Option premiums are placeholders from a reasonable chain; check the live one before doing anything.

The table is the "win rate is a proxy" lesson on my own bet. The cash-secured put wins 80% of the time and has the lowest expected return on capital, because the 20% is where it loses $1,500 against $175 of premium. The call spread has the best return on capital and loses everything 57% of the time. Shares are the boring middle: two-thirds odds, +16% expected, and they pay the dividend while I wait, if it survives. None of this is advice; it's the distribution, priced four ways.

show the code (bet.py, as run)
"""My bet: the value distribution mapped onto the instruments I could actually buy.

The Monte Carlo gives a distribution of what the shares are WORTH. A position pays on
what they TRADE at, and the market can disagree with value for a long time. So the one
extra assumption here is convergence: over the horizon the price moves a fraction c of
the way from $32 toward the simulated value (c = 1 is full convergence, 0 is none).

Option premiums are ILLUSTRATIVE placeholders — check the live chain before trading.
Run: python3 bet.py  (writes bet.json for the post's chart)
"""
import io, contextlib, json
import numpy as np

# re-run the simulation to get the value draws (same seed as montecarlo.py)
src = open("montecarlo.py").read().replace('json.dump(out, open("mc.json", "w"), indent=1)', '')
ns = {}
with contextlib.redirect_stdout(io.StringIO()):
    exec(src, ns)
value, down, price = ns["value"], ns["down"], ns["price"]
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)

# ---- horizon price: partial convergence toward value, plus noise the model can't see
c = 0.6                                              # 60% of the gap closes over ~12–15 months
price_T = price + c * (value - price)
price_T = price_T * np.exp(rng.normal(0, 0.12, len(value)))   # 12% lognormal market noise
price_T = np.maximum(price_T, 3)

# ---- dividend over the horizon: $1.85 held in the base regime, halved in a down-cycle (cut)
div = np.where(down, 0.925, 1.85)

# ---- instruments (per 100 shares / one contract). Premiums are placeholders.
CALL_K, CALL_PREM = 35.0, 3.50          # Jan-2028 $35 call, illustrative
PUT_K, PUT_PREM = 28.0, 1.75            # 6-month $28 cash-secured put, illustrative
SPR_K1, SPR_K2, SPR_NET = 35.0, 45.0, 2.25   # 35/45 call spread, illustrative net debit

instruments = {
    "100 shares at $32": dict(pnl=100 * (price_T - price) + 100 * div, capital=100 * price),
    "1 call, Jan-2028 $35 (~$3.50)": dict(pnl=100 * (np.maximum(0, price_T - CALL_K) - CALL_PREM), capital=100 * CALL_PREM),
    "1 cash-secured put, $28 6-mo (~$1.75)": dict(pnl=100 * (PUT_PREM - np.maximum(0, PUT_K - price_T)), capital=100 * PUT_K),
    "1 call spread 35/45 (~$2.25)": dict(pnl=100 * (np.minimum(SPR_K2 - SPR_K1, np.maximum(0, price_T - SPR_K1)) - SPR_NET), capital=100 * SPR_NET),
}
rows = []
for name, d in instruments.items():
    p = d["pnl"]
    rows.append(dict(name=name, capital=float(d["capital"]), mean=float(p.mean()), p_profit=float((p > 0).mean()),
                     p10=float(np.percentile(p, 10)), p50=float(np.percentile(p, 50)), p90=float(np.percentile(p, 90)),
                     worst=float(p.min()), roc=float(p.mean() / d["capital"]),
                     mean_down=float(p[down].mean()), mean_base=float(p[~down].mean())))
    print(f"{name:40s} capital ${d['capital']:>6,.0f}  E[P&L] ${p.mean():>7,.0f}  P(profit) {(p>0).mean():5.1%}  10th ${np.percentile(p,10):>7,.0f}  90th ${np.percentile(p,90):>7,.0f}  worst ${p.min():>7,.0f}  on capital {p.mean()/d['capital']:+.1%}")

# payoff curves for the chart (per 100 shares, before dividends)
grid = np.arange(10, 70.5, 0.5)
curves = {
    "shares": (100 * (grid - price)).tolist(),
    "call": (100 * (np.maximum(0, grid - CALL_K) - CALL_PREM)).tolist(),
    "put": (100 * (PUT_PREM - np.maximum(0, PUT_K - grid))).tolist(),
    "spread": (100 * (np.minimum(SPR_K2 - SPR_K1, np.maximum(0, grid - SPR_K1)) - SPR_NET)).tolist(),
}
hist, edges = np.histogram(price_T, bins=np.arange(0, 100, 2.5))
json.dump(dict(convergence=c, price=price, grid=grid.tolist(), curves=curves, rows=rows,
               priceT=dict(edges=edges.tolist(), counts=hist.tolist(), p10=float(np.percentile(price_T, 10)), p50=float(np.percentile(price_T, 50)), p90=float(np.percentile(price_T, 90))),
               premiums=dict(call=CALL_PREM, put=PUT_PREM, spread=SPR_NET)), open("bet.json", "w"))
print(f"\nhorizon price (c={c}): 10th ${np.percentile(price_T,10):.0f}  median ${np.percentile(price_T,50):.0f}  90th ${np.percentile(price_T,90):.0f}")
does it tie out

show the Monte Carlo (montecarlo.py, as run)
"""Monte Carlo over the IP valuation chain (same mechanics as the Scenarios sheet).

The deterministic model gives one number per story. This gives the distribution of
value per share when the four decisive assumptions are uncertain, and answers the
question a bet actually needs answered: P(value > price), the tails, and how big a
position the edge justifies (Kelly).

Everything here mirrors IP_valuation_model.xlsx — same inputs, same bridge, same
terminal mechanics. Run: python3 montecarlo.py  (writes mc.json for the post's d3 chart)
"""
import json
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
N = 20000

# ---------------- fixed inputs (Inputs sheet) ----------------
price, shares = 32.00, 529.5
net_debt = 9217 - 726
rev_2025_na, rev_2025_emea = 15000.0, 8500.0
rev_2026_h1 = 11975.0
na_26, emea_26, corp = 2425.0, 950.0, -75.0
price_index_ytd, price_sens, price_in_2026 = 170.0, 9.0, 175.0
contrib_margin = 0.30
costout_27, infl_27, emea_27_delta = 300.0, 250.0, 150.0
rev_growth_lt, da_pct, nwc_pct, tax = 0.025, 0.080, 0.10, 0.24
capex_2027, sep_costs = 1900.0, 250.0
g = 0.02
emea_margin_mid = 0.11
capex_pct_lt = 0.065

rev_na_26 = rev_2026_h1 * 2 * rev_2025_na / (rev_2025_na + rev_2025_emea)
rev_emea_26 = rev_2026_h1 * 2 * rev_2025_emea / (rev_2025_na + rev_2025_emea)
rev_26 = rev_na_26 + rev_emea_26

# ---------------- the uncertain assumptions, with a cycle ----------------
# Independent draws understate the tails: in a downturn price does NOT stick, demand falls,
# margins compress and the multiple shrinks together. So first draw the regime, then draw the
# assumptions conditional on it.  P(down-cycle over the horizon) is itself a judgment: 30%.
p_down = 0.30
down = rng.random(N) < p_down
# 1. Share of the $170/ton that sticks through 2027. Jason: "more like half, lag is months."
stick = np.where(down, rng.beta(2, 5, N), rng.beta(5, 4, N))          # down: mode ~0.2; base: mode ~0.57
# 2. Mid-cycle NA EBITDA margin: SW 13% floor ... PKG 21% ceiling.
na_margin = np.where(down, rng.triangular(0.11, 0.135, 0.16, N), rng.triangular(0.14, 0.175, 0.21, N))
# 3. Exit EV/EBITDA multiple.
mult = np.where(down, rng.triangular(6.0, 7.0, 8.5, N), rng.triangular(7.0, 8.5, 11.0, N))
# 4. WACC: normal around 7.4%, 50 bp sd, floored well above g.
wacc = np.clip(rng.normal(0.074, 0.005, N), 0.055, 0.10)
# 2027 volume: base +1% +/- 1.5%; down-cycle -2.5% +/- 1.5% (TTOBMA's -2% forecast lives here).
vol = np.where(down, rng.normal(-0.025, 0.015, N), rng.normal(0.01, 0.015, N))
# EMEA mid-cycle margin and the 2027 catch-up also depend on the regime.
emea_margin_mid = np.where(down, rng.triangular(0.08, 0.095, 0.11, N), rng.triangular(0.10, 0.115, 0.13, N))
emea_27_delta = np.where(down, rng.normal(0.0, 75.0, N), rng.normal(150.0, 75.0, N))
# Capex intensity 2028+: the 'assets in the ground' lever.
capex_pct = rng.triangular(0.060, 0.065, 0.078, N)
# Model risk: the structure itself is wrong by some amount we cannot see. Lognormal haircut/uplift on
# the terminal value with 15% sd — Damodaran's margin of safety, made explicit instead of applied at the end.
model_noise = np.exp(rng.normal(0.0, 0.15, N))

# ---------------- the chain (vectorized, same as Scenarios sheet) ----------------
price_delta = stick * price_index_ytd * price_sens - price_in_2026
rev_na_27 = rev_na_26 * (1 + vol) + price_delta
rev_emea_27 = rev_emea_26 * (1 + rev_growth_lt)
na_27 = na_26 + price_delta + rev_na_26 * vol * contrib_margin + (costout_27 - infl_27) * rev_na_26 / rev_26
emea_27 = emea_26 + emea_27_delta + (costout_27 - infl_27) * rev_emea_26 / rev_26
ebitda_27 = na_27 + emea_27 + corp
rev_27 = rev_na_27 + rev_emea_27
grow3 = (1 + rev_growth_lt) ** 3
rev_30 = rev_27 * grow3
ebitda_30 = rev_na_27 * grow3 * na_margin + rev_emea_27 * grow3 * emea_margin_mid + corp
ufcf_30 = (ebitda_30 - rev_30 * da_pct) * (1 - tax) + rev_30 * da_pct - rev_30 * capex_pct - rev_30 * rev_growth_lt / (1 + rev_growth_lt) * nwc_pct
ufcf_27 = (ebitda_27 - rev_27 * da_pct) * (1 - tax) + rev_27 * da_pct - capex_2027 - sep_costs * 0.5
pv_interim = (ufcf_27 / (1 + wacc) ** 0.7
              + (ufcf_27 * 2 / 3 + ufcf_30 / 3) / (1 + wacc) ** 1.7
              + (ufcf_27 / 3 + ufcf_30 * 2 / 3) / (1 + wacc) ** 2.7
              + ufcf_30 / (1 + wacc) ** 3.7)
tv = 0.5 * (ufcf_30 * (1 + g) / (wacc - g) + ebitda_30 * mult) * model_noise
ev = pv_interim + tv / (1 + wacc) ** 3.7
value = (ev - net_debt) / shares

# ---------------- what a bet needs to know ----------------
p_above = float((value > price).mean())
pct = {k: float(np.percentile(value, k)) for k in (5, 10, 25, 50, 75, 90, 95)}
ev_value = float(value.mean())
# Treat it as a bet that resolves over ~3 years: return r = value/price - 1 (ignoring the 5.8% dividend,
# which is upside if it survives and a cut if it doesn't). Growth-optimal (Kelly) fraction solves
# max_f E[log(1 + f r)] numerically; we report half of it, which is what practitioners actually size to.
r = value / price - 1
fs = np.linspace(0, 1, 201)
growth = np.array([np.mean(np.log1p(np.clip(f * r, -0.999, None))) for f in fs])
kelly = float(fs[growth.argmax()])
half_kelly = kelly / 2

# what drives it: rank correlation of each input with value
def spearman(a, b):
    ra, rb = a.argsort().argsort(), b.argsort().argsort()
    return float(np.corrcoef(ra, rb)[0, 1])
drivers = {"down-cycle regime": spearman(down.astype(float), value), "price sticks": spearman(stick, value), "NA mid-cycle margin": spearman(na_margin, value),
           "exit multiple": spearman(mult, value), "WACC": spearman(wacc, value),
           "2027 volume": spearman(vol, value), "capex intensity": spearman(capex_pct, value), "model noise": spearman(model_noise, value)}

hist, edges = np.histogram(value, bins=np.arange(0, 100, 2.5))
out = {"n": N, "price": price, "p_down": p_down, "p_above_price_given_down": float((value[down] > price).mean()), "p_above_price_given_base": float((value[~down] > price).mean()),
       "mean_down": float(value[down].mean()), "mean_base": float(value[~down].mean()), "p_above_price": p_above, "mean": ev_value, "percentiles": pct,
       "kelly": kelly, "half_kelly": half_kelly, "drivers": drivers,
       "hist": {"edges": edges.tolist(), "counts": hist.tolist()},
       "p_loss_half": float((value < price * 0.5).mean()), "p_double": float((value > price * 2).mean())}
json.dump(out, open("mc.json", "w"), indent=1)
print(f"P(value > ${price}) = {p_above:.1%}   mean ${ev_value:.1f}   median ${pct[50]:.1f}")
print("percentiles:", {k: round(v, 1) for k, v in pct.items()})
print(f"P(value < half the price) = {out['p_loss_half']:.1%}   P(value > 2x) = {out['p_double']:.1%}")
print(f"Kelly {kelly:.2f}  half-Kelly {half_kelly:.2f}")
print(f"given down-cycle ({p_down:.0%}): P(>price) {out['p_above_price_given_down']:.1%}, mean ${out['mean_down']:.1f};  given base: P(>price) {out['p_above_price_given_base']:.1%}, mean ${out['mean_base']:.1f}")
print("drivers (rank corr):", {k: round(v, 2) for k, v in drivers.items()})

the model and the code

The spreadsheet is IP_valuation_model.xlsx: 289 live formulas, a Dictionary sheet that explains every term in plant language, every input tagged with its source (Q2 2026 release, the Q1 and Q4 calls, Fastmarkets via Packaging Dive, Multiples.vc), and every judgment of mine in yellow so you can overwrite it. The Monte Carlo is montecarlo.py, which mirrors the sheet's scenario chain line for line; build_model.py generates the workbook, validate.py runs the twenty-one tie-out checks, and bet.py maps the distribution onto the instruments. Public information only. Not investment advice; I'm an engineer who builds solvers, not a financial advisor, and I may hold or trade any of these names.

The mechanics are the ones Wall Street Prep teaches — unlevered free cash flow, WACC, a terminal value two ways, comps and sum-of-the-parts. The discipline is Damodaran's: story first, mid-cycle for a cyclical, show what the terminal value carries, and treat the answer as a distribution because that's what it is. The floor is mine.
